The policy toolkit

Three import policies and one export policy. Each opens a wedge between the price at home and the price in the world market; they differ in who collects the wedge.

Tariff

A tax on imports. Specific: a fixed amount per unit ($2.00/lb on milk). Ad valorem: a percent of value (20% on cheese).

Pd = Pw + t  or  Pw(1 + τ)

The wedge is government revenue.

Import quota

A legal limit on the quantity imported. The domestic price rises until home excess demand equals the quota.

ED(Pd) = Q0

The wedge becomes quota rent for whoever holds the licences.

Tariff-rate quota

Imports up to the access limit Ω enter at a low (or zero) tariff; imports above Ω pay a higher over-quota tariff.

Q ≤ Ω: Pw · Q > Ω: Pw + t

The wedge splits into in-quota rent and over-quota revenue.

Export subsidy

A payment per unit exported. Exporters sell abroad for less, so the domestic price rises and the world price falls.

Pd = Pw′ + s

Taxpayers pay the wedge; part of it is a gift to foreign buyers.

Simulator

Home demand Qd = 160 − 1.5P and supply Qs = 20 + 0.5P give import demand Qm = 140 − 2P. A large country trades against foreign excess supply Qx = −60 + 2P. For the export subsidy, the home country is the exporter (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = 40 + P, so Qx = −60 + 2P) facing foreign import demand Qm = 140 − 2P.

Glossary

Specific tariff
A fixed tax per unit imported, e.g. $2.00 per lb.
Ad valorem tariff
A tax set as a percent of the import’s value, e.g. 20% of the price of cheese.
Import quota
A legal ceiling on the quantity that may be imported in a period.
Quota rent
The gap between the domestic and the world price times the quantity imported under the quota. It goes to whoever holds the import licences.
Tariff-rate quota (TRQ)
A two-tier tariff: a low in-quota rate up to the access limit, and a higher over-quota rate beyond it.
Access limit (Ω)
The quantity that may enter at the in-quota tariff under a TRQ.
Export subsidy
A government payment per unit exported. It raises the price exporters receive above the world price.
Excess demand (ED)
Domestic demand minus domestic supply at each price, Qd − Qs. It is the importing country’s demand for imports.
Excess supply (ES)
Domestic supply minus domestic demand, Qs − Qd. It is the exporting country’s supply of exports.
Small country
A trader too small to affect the world price. It faces a horizontal (perfectly elastic) foreign supply or demand curve.
Large country
A trader whose purchases or sales move the world price. It faces an upward-sloping ES (as importer) or downward-sloping ED (as exporter).
Consumer surplus (CS)
The area below the demand curve and above the price paid.
Producer surplus (PS)
The area above the supply curve and below the price received.
Deadweight loss (DWL)
Surplus lost to everyone. Under an import tariff, b is the cost of producing at home units that could have been imported more cheaply, and d is the value of consumption given up.
Terms-of-trade effect
The change in the price a country pays for imports (or receives for exports). For a large importer with a tariff, area e is the part of the tariff paid by foreign exporters through a lower world price.
Optimal tariff
For a large importer, the tariff that maximizes national welfare, where the marginal terms-of-trade gain equals the marginal deadweight loss. It is always zero for a small country.
Tariffication
The Uruguay Round requirement that non-tariff barriers on farm goods, such as quotas, be converted into tariffs, often administered as TRQs.
